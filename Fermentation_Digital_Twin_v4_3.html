Eight-State ODE Model

Active Biomass Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm dt}=\left(\mu_G+\mu_M-k_d-D\right)X\]
The active-cell concentration increases through glycerol- and methanol-associated growth and decreases through first-order loss of viability and hydraulic dilution. The dilution rate is defined as \(D=F_{in}/V\).
Inactive Biomass Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dX_i}{\mathrm dt}=k_dX-DX_i\]
Biomass removed from the active compartment by \(k_dX\) is transferred to an inactive-biomass compartment. This preserves the modelled biomass inventory at the component level, subject to dilution.
Glycerol Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dG}{\mathrm dt}=D\!\left(G_{in}-G\right)-\left(\frac{\mu_G}{Y_{X/G}}+m_G\right)X\]
The first term represents convective glycerol addition and dilution; the second term represents substrate demand for biomass formation and non-growth-associated maintenance. \(G_{in}\) is phase dependent and equals zero when glycerol feed is inactive.
Methanol Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}=D\!\left(M_{in}-M\right)-\left(\frac{\mu_M}{Y_{X/M}}+m_M+\frac{q_P}{Y_{P/M}}\right)X-k_{evap}M\]
Methanol is consumed for growth, maintenance and product formation and is additionally removed by a first-order volatilisation term. The explicit \(q_P/Y_{P/M}\) contribution closes the empirical methanol cost assigned to product synthesis.
Product Balance — Luedeking–Piret Kinetics
\[\frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dt}=q_PX-\left(D+k_{P,deg}\right)P,\qquad q_P=E\left(\alpha\mu+\beta\right)\]
The specific production rate combines a growth-associated contribution \(\alpha\mu\) and a non-growth-associated contribution \(\beta\), modulated by the induction state \(E\). Product concentration decreases through dilution and first-order degradation.
Dissolved-Oxygen Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dC_O}{\mathrm dt}=k_La^{eff}(t)\left(C_O^{\ast}-C_O\right)-r_{O_2}+D\left(C_{O,in}-C_O\right)\]
Liquid-phase oxygen is governed by gas–liquid transfer, cellular uptake \(r_{O_2}\), and oxygen carried by the liquid feed. The effective transfer coefficient is corrected for agitation, aeration and working volume. \(C_O\), \(C_O^{\ast}\), \(C_{O,in}\) and \(K_{DO}\) are evaluated internally in mmol O₂ L⁻¹; charts report dissolved oxygen in mg O₂ L⁻¹. The numerical update is implicit and enforces a non-negative state.
Working-Volume Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=F_G+F_M-F_{out},\qquad F_{out}=0\ \text{for the configured fed-batch process}\]
Working volume is obtained directly from the liquid-flow balance. The equipment limit \(V_{max}\) is retained as a diagnostic threshold rather than imposed as a numerical clamp, thereby preserving the stated balance equation.
Induction-State Balance
\[\frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dt}=k_{ind}\frac{M}{K_{M,ind}+M}\left(1-E\right)-k_{deind}E,\qquad 0\le E\le1\]
\(E\) is a bounded empirical state describing the gradual establishment and loss of methanol-responsive induction. The separate \(K_{M,ind}\) prevents the induction response from being forced to share the methanol-growth half-saturation constant. It is not a direct intracellular concentration and must be identified from induction-sensitive observations.

Kinetic Structure

\[\mu_G=\mu_{G,max}\frac{G}{K_G+G}\frac{C_O}{K_{DO}+C_O}\,f_Tf_{pH}\]
\[\mu_M=\mu_{M,max}\frac{M}{K_M+M+M^2/K_I}\frac{C_O}{K_{DO}+C_O}\,E f_Tf_{pH}\]
\[r_{O_2}^{pot}=\left(\frac{\mu_G}{Y_{X/O_2,G}}+\frac{\mu_M}{Y_{X/O_2,M}}+m_O\right)X\]
\[\mathrm{OTR}=k_La^{eff}(t)\left(C_O^\ast-C_O\right),\qquad \Delta r_{O_2}=\max\!\left(0,r_{O_2}^{pot}-r_{O_2}^{act}\right)\]
\[k_La(t)=\begin{cases}k_La^{B},&t<t_G\\k_La^{G},&t_G\le t<t_M\\k_La^{M},&t\ge t_M\end{cases}\]
\[k_La^{eff}=k_La(t)\left(\frac{N}{N_{ref}}\right)^{a_N}\left(\frac{Q_g}{Q_{ref}}\right)^{a_Q}\left(\frac{V_0}{V}\right)^{a_V}\]

Glycerol growth is represented by multiplicative Monod-type limitation functions. Methanol growth uses a Haldane-type inhibition term and is additionally modulated by the induction state. Temperature and pH enter as empirical activity factors; the displayed CER remains an RQ-based derived variable rather than an independently balanced carbon state.

1. Purpose and Intended Use

The Fermentation Digital Twin website is an interactive research environment for specifying, simulating and interrogating a reduced-order fermentation model. It combines an eight-state ordinary differential equation system with phase-dependent feeding, oxygen-transfer dynamics, multi-batch comparison, data import and diagnostic visualisation. The primary scientific purpose is to make the assumptions and consequences of a candidate model transparent before parameter estimation and experimental validation.

The platform supports three distinct activities: model specification, in which states, kinetic expressions, units and parameter values are reviewed; simulation analysis, in which process trajectories are generated under alternative parameter and feed configurations; and model evaluation, in which experimental observations are superimposed on predictions and summarised using residual, parity and RMSE diagnostics.

Intended-use statement. The website is a research prototype and not a validated process-control or release-decision system. Numerical outputs become scientifically reportable only after the relevant organism, cultivation medium, reactor configuration, sensor units, process phases and parameter-identification procedure have been documented and independently checked.

2. Scientific Scope and Modelling Assumptions

The implemented model is unstructured because intracellular components are not resolved, non-segregated because the cell population is represented by average states, and reduced order because only variables required for the present experimental questions are retained. The liquid phase is assumed to be ideally mixed, so every concentration state is spatially uniform. Feed streams change substrate inventories, dilution and working volume according to the declared process phase.

Active and inactive biomass, glycerol, methanol, product, dissolved oxygen, working volume and induction are explicit states. Glycerol and methanol growth rates are modified by substrate availability, dissolved oxygen, temperature and pH. Product formation follows a Luedeking–Piret relationship. The dissolved-oxygen balance couples cellular oxygen demand to a phase-specific volumetric mass-transfer coefficient.

2.1 State-equation system

\[\begin{aligned} \dot X&=(\mu_G+\mu_M-k_d-D)X\\ \dot X_i&=k_dX-DX_i\\ \dot G&=D(G_{in}-G)-\left(\mu_G/Y_{X/G}+m_G\right)X\\ \dot M&=D(M_{in}-M)-\left(\mu_M/Y_{X/M}+m_M+q_P/Y_{P/M}\right)X-k_{evap}M\\ \dot P&=q_PX-(D+k_{P,deg})P\\ \dot C_O&=k_La^{eff}(t)(C_O^\ast-C_O)-r_{O_2}+D(C_{O,in}-C_O)\\ \dot V&=F_G+F_M-F_{out}\\ \dot E&=k_{ind}\frac{M}{K_{M,ind}+M}(1-E)-k_{deind}E \end{aligned}\]

2.2 Kinetic and production-rate expressions

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_G&=\mu_{G,max}\frac{G}{K_G+G}\frac{C_O}{K_{DO}+C_O}f_Tf_{pH}\\ \mu_M&=\mu_{M,max}\frac{M}{K_M+M+M^2/K_I}\frac{C_O}{K_{DO}+C_O}Ef_Tf_{pH}\\ q_P&=E(\alpha\mu+\beta),\qquad \mu=\mu_G+\mu_M\\ r_{O_2}^{pot}&=\left(\mu_G/Y_{X/O_2,G}+\mu_M/Y_{X/O_2,M}+m_O\right)X \end{aligned}\]

2.3 Numerical implementation

The principal simulation uses a selectable fixed-step RK4 or Euler biological-state update at 0.01 h and an implicit scalar dissolved-oxygen update. Plotted values are sampled every 0.1 h. Monte Carlo and identifiability ensembles use a 0.05 h screening step; Morris screening uses a 0.1 h Euler screening step. The interface compares 0.01 and 0.02 h endpoint results to expose step-size sensitivity. Non-negativity constraints are applied to concentration states, and phase changes select the corresponding feed and base \(k_La\) values before agitation, aeration and volume corrections are applied.

The component balances are physically informed but do not constitute a complete elemental balance. CO₂ is presently derived from oxygen uptake and a phase-weighted respiratory quotient; gas-phase holdup, exhaust-gas composition, heat generation, acid–base equilibria and detailed methanol volatilisation mechanisms are not resolved. These omissions define the current validity domain and should be stated whenever results are presented.

3. Model Configuration and Organism Presets

3.1 Built-in presets

K. phaffii and E. coli are parameter presets. Selecting a preset replaces the shared kinetic, yield, transfer and scheduling parameters with a predefined set, after which the user may edit individual values. Batch Process is a process-configuration template rather than a taxonomic category; it disables the configured feed phases to provide a no-feed reference case.

3.2 Adding another organism

Select Add Organism, enter the scientific or working name, and choose an initial parameter source. “Copy current parameter values” captures the parameter vector currently displayed; the other options copy a built-in preset. The resulting entry is stored locally in the browser and appears beside the built-in presets on subsequent visits from the same browser.

A custom organism entry is a named parameterisation, not a new biological model. Before using it, evaluate whether the organism uses the same carbon sources, whether methanol induction is relevant, whether the Luedeking–Piret structure is defensible, and whether the units and yields correspond to the selected organism. If these assumptions fail, the state equations must be revised rather than merely refitted.

4. Standard Operating Procedure

Step 1 — Define the scientific question

Specify whether the analysis concerns biomass growth, substrate utilisation, oxygen limitation, product formation, batch-to-batch variability or another hypothesis. This determines which states and parameters must be observed and prevents interpreting visually complex plots without a defined question.

Step 2 — Select or create the organism preset

Choose the closest biologically justified preset. For a new organism, add a named preset and treat its copied values only as initial guesses. Record the origin of every fixed or fitted parameter in the experimental notebook or model report.

Step 3 — Verify process phases and initial conditions

Confirm the glycerol-feed start, methanol-induction start, simulation end time, initial biomass, initial glycerol and working volume. Incorrect phase boundaries can create apparently plausible trajectories while assigning kinetics to the wrong operating regime.

Step 4 — Enter parameters and run the simulation

Each numerical parameter supports both slider adjustment and direct typed entry. Use sliders for exploratory sensitivity and typed values for reproducible model specification. Both controls are synchronised and the model reruns after a short delay; Run Simulation forces an immediate update.

Step 5 — Conduct physical plausibility checks

Inspect state trajectories before fit statistics. Confirm non-negative concentrations, credible working volume, plausible substrate depletion, realistic induction timing and a consistent relationship among DO, OTR and oxygen demand. Resolve unit or balance problems before calibration.

Step 6 — Import experimental observations

Use CSV or JSON files with documented units and batch identifiers. Confirm that time is expressed in hours and that DO, biomass, feed and gas-rate units match the model interface. Imported observations are overlaid as points on the corresponding prediction curves.

Step 7 — Evaluate residual structure and cross-batch transferability

Review RMSE together with parity and residual plots. A low aggregate error is insufficient if residuals remain phase dependent or systematically biased. Estimate shared kinetic parameters on a reference batch and evaluate whether they transfer to independent batches with only a restricted set of batch-specific factors.

Step 8 — Freeze and document the analysis

Save a named model-version snapshot before major changes, then export simulated trajectories as CSV and the complete project as JSON. The project file preserves parameters, custom equations, version history, imported and derived datasets, preprocessing records, estimator settings and analysis results.

5. Interface and Page Guide

5.1 Overview

The Overview presents the principal state and rate trajectories for the active parameter set. Background bands denote the Batch, Glycerol Feed and Methanol Induction phases. Summary cards report endpoint or extremum quantities but must be interpreted with the full trajectories. Hovering over a curve reports time and value. Core Parameters contains variables most frequently used in hypothesis testing; Advanced Parameters contains yields, maintenance coefficients, induction constants, degradation terms and environmental corrections.

5.2 Cross-Batch Analysis

Six panels compare R02, R03 and R04 using consistent colours. Solid lines are model predictions. Experimental observations are rendered as scatter points only when the imported batch name and variable are recognised. The right-hand editor applies batch-specific multipliers to the three phase-specific \(k_La\) values, methanol feed rate and methanol biomass yield, with optional initial-condition changes. Shared kinetics remain controlled from Overview.

5.3 Parameter Calibration

The calibration page initially displays a fixed-seed synthetic preview so its configuration, observations and baseline errors remain visible before experimental data are imported. The Demonstration Calibration button illustrates the bounded derivative-free workflow but cannot apply its synthetic fitted parameters to the active model. For reportable work, select an imported batch; errors are scaled within each response and averaged, and the validation selector evaluates the final parameter set on a second batch without refitting. This browser optimizer is intended for initial estimates and workflow screening, not as a substitute for profile likelihood, bootstrap or Bayesian uncertainty analysis.

5.4 Advanced Diagnostics

Growth-rate and kinetic-factor plots identify which mechanisms govern the current trajectory. Oxygen diagnostics compare transfer, actual uptake, potential demand and unmet demand and show the effective \(k_La\) trajectory. Parity and residual plots evaluate agreement with product observations. Local sensitivity reports the change in final product caused by a 10% parameter perturbation. Eight-trajectory Morris screening reports \(\mu^*\) for overall influence and \(\sigma\) for nonlinearity or interaction, while the parameter sensitivity-correlation matrix and approximate condition number flag difficult-to-separate parameter combinations.

5.5 Model & Physics

This page is the formal model specification. Equations are rendered from LaTeX notation and accompanied by an academic interpretation of each term. The state dictionary and scope statement should be reviewed before using a new organism preset, because parameter substitution alone cannot compensate for an invalid state or reaction structure.

5.6 Data Import and recorded inputs

This page accepts CSV and JSON data and supplies a canonical CSV header. A selected dataset can drive the model with time-varying glycerol feed, methanol feed, agitation, aeration, temperature, pH and measured \(k_La\); missing input columns fall back to the parameter panel. Data are processed locally in the current browser session. Clearing the import restores demonstration observations; use Export Project to preserve imported data and settings for later restoration.

5.7 EKF State Estimation

The EKF page performs an offline replay of the selected imported batch. When no dataset has been imported, a fixed-seed synthetic demonstration is generated automatically and labelled throughout the page; it illustrates the workflow but is not experimental evidence. The nonlinear eight-state model propagates between observation times, a finite-difference transition Jacobian propagates the full covariance matrix, and available direct observations of X, G, M, P, DO, V and E are assimilated sequentially. The displayed intervals are filter covariance intervals and depend on the user-specified process and measurement-noise assumptions.

5.8 Custom LaTeX equations

The equation builder parses a restricted mathematical subset of LaTeX and converts the right-hand side into an executable expression tree without evaluating arbitrary JavaScript. It can add a new dynamic state or replace the derivative of X, Xi, G, M, P, V or E. New states are integrated with the selected solver, plotted and exported. A dimensional screen checks declared units and warns about denominator singularities and function domains; numerical constants may still carry implicit units, so this is a screening aid rather than a formal proof. Every structural change requires conservation and validation checks.

5.9 Custom parameters

The Custom Parameter Manager adds named coefficients that can be referenced directly in custom LaTeX equations. Each parameter stores a value, unit, admissible bounds, source note and an optional calibration flag. Selecting the calibration flag exposes the coefficient to the bounded parameter-estimation page. A parameter cannot be removed while an active equation still references it.

5.10 Data preprocessing

The preprocessing workbench creates a derived dataset while preserving the imported source. It supports time conversion and offset, biomass or OD scaling, DO percent-saturation conversion, feed scaling, time-range trimming, negative-concentration screening and duplicate-time averaging. The complete transformation recipe is stored with the derived dataset and is included in the project export.

5.11 Model versions

A model version is a reproducible browser-local snapshot of mechanistic parameters, custom parameters, custom equations, solver choice and batch overrides. Compare reports structural and parameter differences against the active model; Restore replaces the active configuration after confirmation. Project export is still required for durable transfer between browsers or computers.

5.12 Moving-horizon estimation

The MHE page performs constrained offline rolling-window estimation of the eight core states. At each observation time, it minimizes a normalized robust Huber objective over the selected recent horizon while enforcing non-negativity, induction bounds and reactor-volume bounds. The present coordinate-search implementation is a research prototype for estimator comparison and workflow design; it is not yet a real-time nonlinear-programming controller. Results should be compared with open-loop predictions, EKF estimates and withheld measurements.

8. Experimental-Data Requirements and Import Procedure

At minimum, each dataset requires a time vector and one measured variable. For calibration, the preferred set includes biomass or OD with a documented conversion, glycerol and methanol concentrations, product concentration, DO, cumulative or instantaneous feed, working volume, agitation, airflow and off-gas O₂/CO₂. Record sensor calibration, sampling time, analytical method and missing-value convention.

  1. Standardise time: convert all online and offline signals to hours relative to a declared batch start and retain original timestamps separately.
  2. Standardise units: convert DO to mg O₂ L⁻¹ for import or document any percentage-saturation conversion. Confirm whether gas rates are molar, volumetric or normalised.
  3. Define batch identity: use R02, R03 and R04 when observations should overlay the corresponding default comparison batches. Other names remain importable but will not be assigned automatically.
  4. Represent missing measurements: leave cells empty rather than inserting zeros. A zero is interpreted as a physical observation.
  5. Verify phase metadata: ensure feed starts and induction times in the model correspond to the experimental event log.
  6. Inspect before fitting: plot raw and processed data, check duplicated timestamps, abrupt unit changes, sensor dropouts and impossible values.
Data-governance recommendation. Keep raw data immutable. Perform unit conversion, filtering and alignment in a documented preprocessing step, and preserve a machine-readable record of every transformation used to create the imported dataset.

9. Validation Metrics and Diagnostic Interpretation

9.1 RMSE and cross-batch performance

RMSE summarises the typical magnitude of prediction error in the native unit of each variable. It must not be compared directly across variables with different units or scales. Report the number of matched observations and preferably include a normalised error, confidence interval or measurement-error benchmark. Cross-batch validation is stronger evidence than refitting every batch independently.

9.2 Parity and residual plots

Parity points near the identity line indicate numerical agreement but do not prove structural validity. A slope different from one may indicate calibration or unit scaling error. Time-ordered residuals should be examined for phase-dependent bias, autocorrelation, heteroscedasticity and delayed dynamics. Structured residuals suggest missing mechanisms or an incorrect event schedule.

9.3 Oxygen supply and demand

Interpret DO, OTR, Actual OUR, Potential Oxygen Demand and Oxygen Deficit together. A fall in DO is expected when cellular demand approaches transfer capacity. Near-zero \(K_{DO}\) sensitivity under oxygen-replete conditions indicates insufficient excitation of the oxygen-limitation term, not universal irrelevance of \(K_{DO}\).

9.4 Sensitivity and uncertainty screening

The local chart perturbs one parameter by +10% while holding all others fixed and reports the percentage change in final product. Morris screening uses eight deterministic trajectories across bounded parameter ranges and combines final biomass, product, minimum DO and maximum methanol. \(\mu^*\) ranks overall influence and \(\sigma\) flags nonlinear or interaction effects. The parameter sensitivity-correlation matrix and its condition number flag parameter pairs that may be difficult to separate. The 90% trajectory bands propagate user-specified independent parameter uncertainty through a fixed-seed Monte Carlo ensemble; they are not experimentally calibrated confidence intervals.

9.5 Trajectory correlation

The heatmap contains Pearson correlations among raw state trajectories. Monotonic trajectories can correlate strongly because both follow time, creating spurious association. The matrix is therefore exploratory only and must not be interpreted as causality, stoichiometric coupling or parameter identifiability.

10. Recommended Parameter-Estimation Strategy

  1. Fix measured inputs first. Use feed records, working volume, temperature, pH and saturation concentration as measured inputs wherever possible.
  2. Establish the glycerol phase. Estimate \(\mu_{G,max}\), \(K_G\), \(Y_{X/G}\) and maintenance using non-induced biomass and glycerol observations. Do not fit methanol parameters in this stage.
  3. Constrain oxygen transfer and respiration. Use DO, airflow, agitation and off-gas data to estimate phase-specific \(k_La\), oxygen yields and \(m_O\). Check the complete oxygen unit chain.
  4. Identify methanol kinetics. Use residual methanol and biomass data across a range that includes both low-concentration limitation and, if safe and experimentally justified, inhibition. Otherwise fix \(K_I\) from independent evidence.
  5. Identify induction and product formation last. Estimate \(k_{ind}\), \(k_{deind}\), \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(Y_{P/M}\) and product degradation only after growth and substrate kinetics are constrained.
  6. Separate estimation from validation. Fit shared kinetics on designated training batches, then evaluate predictions on independent batches. Restrict batch-specific parameters to quantities with a plausible physical source of variation.
  7. Quantify uncertainty. Report confidence intervals, parameter correlations, profile likelihoods or posterior distributions rather than only a single optimum.

11. Model Limitations and Appropriate Claims

  • Default parameters and demonstration observations are illustrative and must not be presented as experimentally calibrated results.
  • The model does not close elemental carbon, hydrogen and oxygen balances because CO₂, water and all by-products are not independent states.
  • Temperature and pH act through empirical correction factors; no energy balance, acid–base equilibrium or base-addition balance is solved.
  • The empirical agitation, aeration and volume correction for effective kLa must be calibrated for the selected vessel and operating range.
  • The well-mixed assumption excludes spatial gradients, mixing time and scale-dependent transfer limitations.
  • The induction state is empirical and may not transfer to an organism or promoter system with different regulation.
  • The Luedeking–Piret relationship is an empirical production law. Its suitability must be evaluated for the selected product and expression system.
  • Sensitivity screens and assumed-uncertainty bands do not establish formal identifiability or experimental confidence.
  • Adding a custom organism does not modify substrates, state definitions or stoichiometry. Structural adaptation is required when the biological system differs materially.

Minimum information to report with results

A scientific report or presentation should state the organism and strain, reactor and working volume, medium and carbon sources, process phases, measured inputs, state definitions, complete parameter table with units and sources, numerical integration settings, calibration objective, parameter constraints, training and validation batches, data preprocessing, observation counts, error metrics and the principal limitations listed above. Figures should distinguish predictions from observations and identify whether observations are experimental or demonstration data.
